Attention Sinks: the softmax has no off switch

Every attention row has to sum to one. When a head has nothing worth attending to it still has to put that weight somewhere, so the model builds itself a drain.

Naman Jaswani·13 August 2026·~12 min softmaxKV cacheinterpretability

I keep coming back to one experiment because it still feels wrong.

When an LLM generates text, every new token attends to every previous one. Rather than recompute those, you cache each token's key and value vectors. That store is the KV cache, and it grows linearly with the sequence, so on an endless stream it eventually eats all your memory.

The obvious fix is to cap it. Keep, say, the most recent 1024 tokens and evict anything older. Reasonable enough, since attention is mostly local anyway.

Perplexity explodes. Not gracefully: it collapses, and the output turns to gibberish.

That is the part that feels wrong. The window still holds 1024 tokens of perfectly good recent context, and nothing about the local information got worse. The model should be able to predict the next word from the last thousand words, and it cannot.

Now change one thing. Pin positions 0 to 3 in the cache permanently, alongside the sliding window, so the cache holds 4 + 1024 instead of 1024. Quality comes back, and you can stream millions of tokens.

Four tokens. Not four important tokens, just the first four, whatever they happened to be. Position 0 is usually only <BOS>, which carries no content at all. They cannot be supplying information the model needs, because there is no information in them to supply.

So the fix cannot be an information story. It has to be a mechanical one, and that mechanism turns out to matter for KV-cache design, for quantization, for why prompts break when you trim them from the front, and for whether attention maps tell you anything at all.

Softmax forces a choice

Attention weights come out of a row-wise softmax:

a[i,j] = exp(q_i · k_j / √d) / Σ_l exp(q_i · k_l / √d) Σ_j a[i,j] = 1 ← the whole problem

The denominator normalises so that each row sums to exactly one. Every query position gets one unit of attention to spend, and it spends all of it.

Now think about what a single head actually does. Heads are specialists. One tracks subject-verb agreement, one copies from an earlier mention of the same entity, one finds the matching open bracket. Being a specialist means being conditional: the head has work to do only when its pattern is present.

So what does an induction head do at a position with nothing to induct from? Under softmax, "nothing" is not on the menu. The row still has to sum to one, so the head has to put its unit of weight somewhere.

That is the whole asymmetry. There is no slot for "none of these". No way to emit a row that sums to 0.3, or 0.03, or nothing at all. A head that has searched the context and found nothing worth reading is not permitted to report that. It is only permitted to say where. Every head, at every position, in every layer, on every forward pass any transformer has ever run, is compelled to point at something and hand over its full unit of weight.

Attention has no do-not-attend-to-any option.

Nobody ever shipped that flag. So the model went and built one.

Why the first token, specifically

Two things make position 0 the obvious parking spot, and both fall out of causal masking.

  1. Everyone can see it. With a causal mask, token 0 sits in the receptive field of every query position. It is the only key you can count on being there no matter where you are in the sequence. If a head wants one designated no-op target that always works, there is exactly one candidate.
  2. Training reinforces it. Early on, attention is close to uniform. Early positions have almost nothing to choose from, since token 1 can only look at tokens 0 and 1, so token 0 collects a lot of weight by default and gradient from every downstream position flows back into it. Its key drifts toward a direction that scores well against any query, which makes it a better place to park, which sends more weight its way.

One thing worth being clear about: the sink attaches to the position, not to the token. Put an arbitrary word at index 0 and the sink stays there. Newlines, periods and other high-frequency structural tokens pick up secondary sinks for the same reason, since they are common, content-free and visible from a lot of places. The Qualcomm group put a number on this: over 97% of outlier activations in the models they examined sit on whitespace and punctuation positions.

Figure 2 shows this. Two things first, because attention matrices are easy to misread.

How to read it. Each row is one token doing the reading. Each column is a token being read from. The cell where they cross is how much of that row's one unit of weight went there. The top right half is blank because a token cannot read the future: token 3 has no access to token 7. That also means row 0 has only one place to look, itself, so it sits at 1.00 no matter what the head would prefer.

What an induction head does. It spots when something is happening for the second time, and copies what happened the first time.

Suppose the text so far reads Ada Lovelace wrote the first program. Ada Lovelace wrote the. A head sitting on that last the looks back for the earlier the, sees that first came straight after it, and attends to first. Its guess for the next word is first, because that is what followed last time.

That sentence is the sequence in the figure. Said once, then started again.

What a sinking head actually outputs

The observation usually gets stated as: for a sinking head, softmax(QKᵀ)V ≈ const, so the head adds no information.

First half right, second half not quite. If a head puts 95% of its weight on the sink then its output is roughly v_sink, the same vector no matter which query position you are at. That is not zero information. It is zero token-dependent information. What the head writes into the residual stream is a constant, which is a bias term.

out_i = Σ_j a[i,j] · v_j a[i,0] ≈ 1 ⇒ out_i ≈ v_0 for all i constant in i ⇒ a bias, not a signal, and not zero

Three reasons the distinction matters:

  • Downstream layers can learn to ignore a constant, or subtract it, or use it as a bias. Nothing breaks.
  • It still is not free. The model is spending a KV slot, a set of head parameters and some residual-stream norm to emulate a bias nobody gave it.
  • In trained models ‖v_sink‖ is often small, which makes the no-op cleaner still. A large weight on a small value vector is a small perturbation. The sail-sg study puts it more strongly: the sink behaves like a key bias, absorbing spare attention while contributing almost nothing to the value computation.

And this is per head per position, not per layer. A layer where half the heads sink and half fire is doing plenty of work. The accurate version is: this head, at this position, is idling.

There is a competing reading. The sink may not be a pure no-op. People working on vision transformers found the same shape of thing, a few high-norm artifact tokens showing up in low-information background patches, and read them as scratch space: the model reusing uninformative positions to hold global information. Giving it dedicated register tokens to do that in cleaned up the attention maps and helped dense prediction. So sinks may be doing two jobs at once. The two readings are not exclusive, and which one dominates probably depends on the head.

Three fixes, increasing invasiveness

1 · Add a phantom zero-logit key

Evan Miller's take in Attention Is Off By One is that none of this is a design tradeoff. It is a bug, and the fix is one character.

softmax(x)_i = exp(x_i) / ( Σ_j exp(x_j)) softmax₁(x)_i = exp(x_i) / ( 1 + Σ_j exp(x_j)) ↑ the missing term

Adding 1 = exp(0) to the denominator is the same thing as appending a phantom key whose logit is 0 and whose value vector is 0. Rows can now come to less than one. A head with nothing to do sends its weight to the phantom and writes approximately nothing into the residual stream.

That reading is Miller's own. He nearly called the function ghostmax, on the grounds that you can think of an extra zero-valued entry in the logits, since exp(0) = 1, together with a zero row in V that attenuates the result. He named the resulting mechanism QuietAttention, because it lets attention heads keep quiet.

The name earns itself once you ask where softmax came from. Softmax is the right tool for picking exactly one of n options, which is what multinomial classification does. Attention is not doing that. Attention is an optional lookup, and the options it should be choosing between are not the n keys but the n keys plus the option of picking none of them. It was handed a list of n and it normalised over n. The list should have been n + 1.

Which gives you the cleanest way to read the sink. The model reinvented this fix on its own, using what it had. With no phantom slot available it took a real one: position 0, the only key visible from everywhere.

Flagging this ahead of time Miller called his post an opinion piece, ran no experiments, and asked readers to test it. So I did, at toy scale: a small model trained with softmax₁ built a sink on position 0 anyway, 0.448 against 0.447 for plain softmax, with the phantom slot absorbing only 0.023. That is not a refutation. His prediction was about quantization outliers and weight kurtosis, not about where attention parks, and 2 layers at 64 dimensions is nobody's idea of evidence. But read the next three fixes as theory with an open experimental question attached.

It is worth noticing how the diagnosis arrived, because it was not three teams stumbling on the same thing at once. A group at Qualcomm AI Research found it first, from the quantization side, chasing activation outliers that were wrecking low-precision inference. Their paper carries a title that doubles as the thesis of this post: Quantizable Transformers: Removing Outliers by Helping Attention Heads Do Nothing. They traced the outliers to the softmax and proposed two fixes, a clipped softmax and a gated attention variant. Miller read that paper, judged both fixes overbuilt, and pointed out that the same job is done by incrementing a denominator. He is explicit that they got "killer-in-the-closet close" to the off-by-one. StreamingLLM then found the same object from a third direction two months later, as the thing you must not evict.

What breaking Σ = 1 actually changes

It is easy to read +1 as a cosmetic change to a normaliser. It is not. Three things change, and the second one is the one people skip past.

The output stops being a convex combination of value vectors. Under softmax, out_i is always a weighted average of the v_j, so it sits inside their convex hull and cannot be small unless the values themselves are small. Under softmax₁ the row is a sub-distribution, a measure with total mass somewhere in [0, 1] rather than a probability distribution, so the output can shrink continuously toward the origin. Heads write additively into the residual stream, so a near-zero write leaves the stream alone. That is the do-nothing operation the architecture never provided.

Translation invariance goes away, and that is the point. Softmax does not care if you shift every logit by the same constant, since softmax(z + c) = softmax(z). Only the differences between logits carry information, which means a softmax head literally cannot say "none of these keys are a good match". All it can say is "this one beats that one". Softmax₁ compares logits against a fixed reference of 0, so absolute size starts to matter. The head gets a dimension it did not have before: not just which key, but whether any key at all.

How Miller puts it He does not use the phrase translation invariance. He makes the same point as a limit, which is arguably cleaner: as every logit goes to minus infinity, ordinary softmax converges to 1/k per key and never releases its unit of weight, while softmax₁ converges to zero. He calls that "an escape hatch in the negative orthant", and separately notes that the ratio between any two output weights is identical under both functions, so nothing about the head's preferences is disturbed. Only its ability to decline changes.

You inherit a numerical stability trap. The usual max-subtraction trick has to account for the phantom logit, which means clamping the max at zero. Without that, a row of strongly negative logits overflows the exp(-m) term.

def softmax1(x, dim=-1):
    # exp(x_i) / (1 + sum_j exp(x_j)), which is a softmax over x
    # with a zero logit appended whose value vector is also zero.
    m = x.amax(dim=dim, keepdim=True).clamp(min=0.0)   # phantom logit is 0
    e = (x - m).exp()
    return e / ((-m).exp() + e.sum(dim=dim, keepdim=True))

Note the clamp(min=0.0). Leave it out and the function is fine for ordinary logits and silently wrong for exactly the case it exists to handle, the idle head whose logits are all low.

2 · Make the null logit learnable

a[i,j] = exp(z_ij) / (exp(s_h) + Σ_l exp(z_il))

Same idea, but the null logit is a trainable scalar per head. Each head learns its own threshold for giving up. Try the learned sink logit mode in Figure 1 and sweep s_h. It is a dial running from plain softmax to writing nothing.

This is not hypothetical. OpenAI's gpt-oss ships it, and the implementation is almost exactly the sentence above:

# gpt_oss/torch/model.py, lightly trimmed
self.sinks = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.empty(config.num_attention_heads))
...
QK = torch.cat([QK, S], dim=-1)     # append the per-head sink logit
W = torch.softmax(QK, dim=-1)
W = W[..., :-1]                     # then drop that column

Append a phantom logit, softmax over the widened row, throw the phantom away. One learnable scalar per head, and the row of real weights is free to sum to less than one. The same file also applies a sliding window on alternate layers, so a shipped model is carrying both halves of this post: the cache trick from the top, and the mechanism that makes it unnecessary.

3 · Drop normalisation entirely

a[i,j] = σ(q_i · k_j / √d + b)

Each key gets gated on its own. No competition between keys, no row-sum constraint, no forced weight. Attending to nothing is directly representable, so the pressure that creates the sink is gone at the source. The sail-sg/Attention-Sink line of work is the reference point here, and it reports that models trained with sigmoid attention show no attention sink at all, and that this still holds when scaled to 1B parameters.

It is not a drop-in swap though.

  • You give up the competitive prior. "These keys compete for a fixed budget" is a genuinely useful thing to build in, and losing it is not obviously free.
  • Output magnitude now grows with sequence length, so you need a length-dependent bias, roughly b ≈ -log n, to keep activations in range.
  • Initialisation needs care for training to stay stable, and you need decent kernels to keep pace with FlashAttention.
  • The published no-sink results are at modest scale. Whether they hold up at frontier scale is open.

Why practitioners care

The sink stops being a curiosity the moment you touch the KV cache. Move the cursor and toggle the retention policy.

  • Cache eviction. Any sliding-window or compression scheme has to pin the first few tokens. That is the StreamingLLM result, and it is why eviction policies special-case position 0.
  • Prompt truncation. Trimming an overlong prompt from the front can hurt much more than trimming from the middle, because you might delete the sink.
  • Quantization. Sinks travel with massive activations, outlier features of enormous magnitude sitting on a few tokens and channels. They ruin naive per-tensor activation quantization, and they are much of the reason per-channel and group-wise scaling exist. This is also how the sink got found in the first place. If your INT8 conversion is losing accuracy for no visible reason, suspect the sink before you suspect your calibration set.
  • Interpretability. A large attention weight does not mean the token is important. A head dumping 95% of its weight on <BOS> is telling you it is inactive, not that <BOS> is critical. Attention-as-explanation has this backwards, and any attribution method built on raw weights needs to handle sinks deliberately.

The takeaway

Attention sinks are not a bug the model has. They are a workaround the model built. Softmax handed it a constraint, spend exactly one unit of attention, with no way out, and gradient descent found the cheapest way out available: pick a token everyone can see and make it the place where unwanted weight goes to die.

Which makes the interesting question something other than how to remove attention sinks. It is what else the architecture has forced the model to improvise, and what those improvisations are costing us.
